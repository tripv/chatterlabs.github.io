🎩 Mr. Chatterbox

We introduce the Mr. Chatterbox model family, a family of historical language models trained on 19th-century British text. The family consists of four fully-trained base models — Mr.Chatterbox-345M, Mr.Chatterbox-500M, Mr.Chatterbox-1.2B and Mr.Chatterbox-2.7B — as well as Mr.Chatterbox-2.7B-Chat, a chat-tuned version of 2.7B trained on fine-tuning data drawn from the corpus. We are also releasing the fine-tuning dataset, Mr.Chatterbox-SFT, and the training code on GitHub.

Sources

The pretraining corpus draws on text from these sources, restricted to 1800–1899:

  • HathiTrust
  • Institutional Books 1.0
  • British Library (blbooks)
  • Project Gutenberg
  • Internet Archive (National Library of Scotland, UK Medical Heritage Library, Wellcome Collection, European libraries)
  • Encyclopaedia Britannica
  • NCSE
  • Old Bailey Proceedings
  • Hansard
  • House of Commons “Blue Books”

Institutional Books provided by the Institutional Data Initiative with source material contributed by Harvard Library, available at institutional.org/institutional-books.

Held-out perplexity

All four models were scored on the same 2,000 sequences of 2,048 tokens each.

  • Sample: drawn uniformly at random (seed 42) from the 141,185 packed sequences in the held-out split. Its source mix matches the corpus: 66.7% HathiTrust, 8.4% Wellcome, 4.3% British Library, and so on.
  • Scoring: fp32 weights, scored one sequence at a time. Loss on the [year][source][ocr] prefix tokens is masked, as in training.
  • Confidence intervals: 95%, from the per-sequence loss.
  • Bits per byte (BPB): total loss (in bits) divided by the UTF-8 bytes of the predicted text. Unlike perplexity, it doesn’t depend on the tokenizer.

Supervised fine-tuning corpus

Mr.Chatterbox-2.7B-Chat was fine-tuned on 19,800 conversations containing 46,719 assistant replies; 7,016 of the conversations are multi-turn. Almost every reply is real 19th-century text. In 56.6% of turns, both the question and the reply were taken from period text (catechisms, novel dialogue, play dialogue, etc.). Most of the remaining user turns were back-translated: written to fit a real Victorian passage. The data is released as Mr.Chatterbox-SFT, and the methods that built it are documented, with code, in the GitHub repository.

Privacy, content and licence

  • Real visitor prompts: many prompts were typed by visitors to the Mr. Chatterbox chatbot, who were not told their words might be published. Short or stock prompts (“Tell me a joke”, “Good evening, sir”) are kept verbatim; every other visitor prompt is replaced by a stand-in that does the same job but carries no identifying detail, and real names are swapped for invented ones. These prompts are therefore not identical to the ones the model was trained on.
  • Removed: 138 conversations containing text from Institutional Books 1.0, whose terms forbid redistribution, and one conversation whose prompts could not be rewritten safely. The released text is the clean version, not the copy with simulated OCR noise the model was also trained on.
  • Content warning: the replies are period text and reproduce 19th-century attitudes on race, empire, religion, gender and class. Many user turns are deliberately abusive, sexual, bigoted or jailbreak attempts: the data exists to teach the model how to respond to them.
  • Licence: CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, matching the most restrictive licensed source. The full source list, record format and citation are on the dataset card.

Benchmarks

Mr.Chatterbox 2.7B-Base and Mr.Chatterbox 2.7B-Chat were both evaluated by us using the Chronologic benchmark for historical language models, released by AICHR (Artificial Intelligence for Cultural and Historical Research). Chronologic tests whether a model can answer as a writer of a given date would. It has 866 questions drawn from texts published between 1831 and 1930. We scored the released 2.7B base model and the 2.7B chat model with the benchmark’s own likelihood scorer. Only the model loader was adapted, for our tokenizer and chat format.

Rows in bold are ours. The other rows are the benchmark authors’ published results. Brier is calibrated, as on the leaderboard (median of 1,000 bootstrap draws); lower is better.

  • The 2.7B base model is second on the leaderboard, behind talkie-1930-13b-base. Their 95% intervals overlap.
  • Our corpus ends in 1899, but 38% of the eval questions (329) come from texts published 1900–1930. Talkie 1930 was trained on text up to 1930. Even so, overall accuracy barely moves across the line (35.2% before 1900, 34.3% after), because most questions test period language and reasoning rather than knowledge of a particular text. The cutoff does show on the questions that need post-1899 knowledge: the base model answers none of the 16 post-1900 knowledge questions correctly.
  • Dialogue fine-tuning roughly halves knowledge-and-inference accuracy (44.4% → 23.8%). Talkie shows a similar drop from base to instruction-tuned (44.0% → 25.1%). In our case the loss is almost entirely on the abstention questions, where the right answer is to decline. The base model declines on 56 of 62, the chat model on 7, because Mr. Chatterbox is trained to stay in character and answer. On the knowledge questions themselves, the chat model does slightly better than the base (35 of 94 correct, against 27).
  • Overlap with our corpus: at least 19 of the benchmark’s 213 source books (9%) are also in our pretraining corpus, matched by library barcode. Other editions were not checked, so this is a lower bound.
